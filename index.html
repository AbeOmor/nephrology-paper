Chronic Kidney Disease: Pathophysiology, Diagnosis, and Emerging Therapeutic Approaches

A Review Paper in Nephrology
📄 Review Article | ~3,500 words | April 2026

Abstract

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately 850 million people worldwide and remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally. Defined by a sustained reduction in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) below 60 mL/min/1.73 m² or evidence of kidney damage persisting for three or more months, CKD progresses through five stages culminating in end-stage renal disease (ESRD), where renal replacement therapy becomes necessary for survival. This paper provides a comprehensive overview of nephrology as a discipline, examines the pathophysiology and staging of CKD, reviews current diagnostic approaches and treatment modalities, and explores emerging therapeutic strategies including SGLT2 inhibitors, novel biomarkers, and advances in regenerative medicine.

Keywords: nephrology, chronic kidney disease, glomerular filtration rate, dialysis, kidney transplantation, SGLT2 inhibitors

1. Introduction

Nephrology, derived from the Greek nephros (kidney) and logos (study), is the branch of internal medicine concerned with the diagnosis and treatment of kidney diseases. The kidneys perform essential functions including waste filtration, electrolyte balance, blood pressure regulation through the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), erythropoietin production, and vitamin D metabolism. When these functions deteriorate, the consequences are systemic and potentially fatal.

The field of nephrology emerged as a distinct specialty in the mid-twentieth century, catalyzed by the development of hemodialysis by Willem Kolff in 1943 and the first successful kidney transplant by Joseph Murray in 1954 (Kolff, 1965; Murray et al., 1958). Since then, nephrology has expanded to encompass glomerular diseases, acute kidney injury (AKI), hereditary nephropathies, electrolyte disorders, and hypertension management.

Chronic kidney disease has reached epidemic proportions. The Global Burden of Disease Study estimated that CKD caused 1.4 million deaths in 2021, a figure that has risen 40% since 2000 (GBD Chronic Kidney Disease Collaboration, 2024). Diabetes mellitus and hypertension account for approximately 70% of CKD cases in developed nations, while infectious etiologies including HIV-associated nephropathy and schistosomiasis remain significant in low-income countries (Jha et al., 2013).

2. Kidney Anatomy and Physiology

2.1 Structural Organization

Each kidney contains approximately one million nephrons — the functional units responsible for filtration, reabsorption, and secretion. A nephron consists of the glomerulus (a capillary tuft enclosed by Bowman's capsule) and a tubular system comprising the proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule, and collecting duct.

Blood enters the glomerulus via the afferent arteriole and exits through the efferent arteriole. The glomerular filtration barrier — comprising fenestrated endothelial cells, the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), and podocyte foot processes — permits the passage of water and small solutes while retaining proteins and blood cells.

2.2 Key Functions

  • Filtration and excretion: The kidneys filter approximately 180 liters of plasma daily, producing 1–2 liters of urine containing metabolic waste products including urea, creatinine, and uric acid.
  • Electrolyte homeostasis: Precise regulation of sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphate, and magnesium concentrations through tubular reabsorption and secretion.
  • Acid-base balance: Renal tubular cells regenerate bicarbonate and excrete hydrogen ions, maintaining blood pH within the narrow range of 7.35–7.45.
  • Endocrine functions: Production of erythropoietin (stimulating red blood cell production), activation of vitamin D (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D₃), and secretion of renin (regulating blood pressure and fluid volume).

3. Chronic Kidney Disease: Pathophysiology

3.1 Mechanisms of Progression

Regardless of the initial insult, CKD progression follows a common pathway. Damage to a critical number of nephrons triggers compensatory hyperfiltration in the remaining nephrons, mediated by increased intraglomerular pressure. While initially adaptive, sustained hyperfiltration causes podocyte injury, proteinuria, and progressive glomerulosclerosis (Brenner, 1985).

This maladaptive response activates several pathological cascades:

  • RAAS activation: Angiotensin II promotes vasoconstriction, inflammation, and fibrosis. Aldosterone contributes to sodium retention and further fibrotic remodeling.
  • Tubulointerstitial fibrosis: Proteinuria activates tubular epithelial cells, which release pro-inflammatory cytokines and undergo epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition — the strongest histological predictor of CKD progression (Nangaku, 2006).
  • Oxidative stress: Mitochondrial dysfunction and NADPH oxidase activation generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), damaging cellular structures and amplifying inflammation.
  • Chronic inflammation: Elevated levels of C-reactive protein, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha are consistently observed in CKD patients and correlate with cardiovascular risk.

3.2 Staging and Classification

The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) classification system stages CKD based on GFR and albuminuria:

Albuminuria categories (A1: <30 mg/g, A2: 30–300 mg/g, A3: >300 mg/g) further refine risk stratification. The combination of GFR and albuminuria categories determines prognosis and guides treatment intensity (KDIGO, 2024).

4. Diagnosis

4.1 Serum Creatinine and Estimated GFR

Serum creatinine remains the most widely used marker for kidney function. GFR is estimated using equations such as the CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) formula, which incorporates age, sex, and serum creatinine. In 2021, the CKD-EPI equation was updated to remove the race coefficient, reflecting concerns about the biological validity of race-based adjustments (Inker et al., 2021).

4.2 Cystatin C

Cystatin C, a low-molecular-weight protein freely filtered by the glomerulus, is less affected by muscle mass than creatinine and provides a more accurate GFR estimate in certain populations, including the elderly, malnourished, and those with extremes of body composition.

4.3 Urinalysis and Albuminuria

Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) is the standard screening test for albuminuria. Persistent albuminuria (≥30 mg/g on two of three samples over three months) indicates glomerular damage and is an independent risk factor for CKD progression and cardiovascular disease.

4.4 Imaging

Renal ultrasonography is the first-line imaging modality, assessing kidney size, cortical thickness, and hydronephrosis. Small, echogenic kidneys suggest chronic disease, while normal-sized kidneys may indicate an acute or reversible process. CT angiography and MR angiography are used for vascular evaluation, while kidney biopsy remains the gold standard for histological diagnosis of glomerular diseases.

4.5 Novel Biomarkers

Emerging biomarkers under investigation include:

  • Kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1): Elevated in proximal tubular injury
  • Neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL): Early marker of acute kidney injury with prognostic value in CKD
  • Soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR): Associated with focal segmental glomerulosclerosis and CKD progression
  • Urinary epidermal growth factor (uEGF): Reflects tubular regenerative capacity and predicts CKD progression independently of GFR and albuminuria (Ju et al., 2015)

5. Treatment

5.1 Conservative Management

Blood pressure control: RAAS inhibition with ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) is the cornerstone of CKD management, reducing proteinuria and slowing progression. Target blood pressure is generally <130/80 mmHg for patients with albuminuria (KDIGO, 2024).

Glycemic control: In diabetic nephropathy, HbA1c targets of <7% reduce microvascular complications, though individualization is essential in advanced CKD where hypoglycemia risk increases.

Dietary modification: Protein restriction (0.6–0.8 g/kg/day) may slow progression in stages G3–G5. Sodium restriction (<2 g/day) supports blood pressure control. Potassium and phosphate restriction become necessary in advanced stages.

Management of complications:

  • Anemia: Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) and iron supplementation, targeting hemoglobin 10–11.5 g/dL
  • Mineral bone disease: Phosphate binders, active vitamin D analogs, and calcimimetics to manage secondary hyperparathyroidism
  • Metabolic acidosis: Oral sodium bicarbonate when serum bicarbonate falls below 22 mmol/L
  • Cardiovascular risk: Statins (in non-dialysis CKD), smoking cessation, and weight management

5.2 Renal Replacement Therapy

Hemodialysis: The most common modality worldwide, typically performed three times weekly for 3–4 hours per session. Blood is circulated through a semipermeable membrane dialyzer where waste products and excess fluid are removed. Arteriovenous fistula is preferred for vascular access.

Peritoneal dialysis: Uses the peritoneal membrane as the dialysis surface. Advantages include home-based treatment, preservation of residual renal function, and greater patient autonomy.

Kidney transplantation: The optimal treatment for ESRD, offering superior survival and quality of life compared to dialysis. Living donor transplantation provides the best outcomes, with 10-year graft survival rates exceeding 70%. Barriers include organ shortage, immunological compatibility, and lifelong immunosuppression.

6. Emerging Therapeutic Approaches

6.1 SGLT2 Inhibitors

Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have emerged as transformative therapies in nephrology. The DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY trials demonstrated that dapagliflozin and empagliflozin reduced the composite risk of sustained GFR decline, ESRD, and renal death by 39% and 28%, respectively, in both diabetic and non-diabetic CKD patients (Heerspink et al., 2020; The EMPA-KIDNEY Collaborative Group, 2023).

SGLT2 inhibitors are now recommended by KDIGO for all CKD patients with eGFR ≥20 mL/min/1.73 m² and albuminuria ≥200 mg/g, regardless of diabetic status.

6.2 Non-Steroidal Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists

Finerenone demonstrated significant reductions in CKD progression and cardiovascular events in the FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO-DKD trials (Bakris et al., 2020; Pitt et al., 2021). Unlike steroidal MRAs, finerenone has lower rates of hyperkalemia and is specifically indicated for diabetic kidney disease.

6.3 Endothelin Receptor Antagonists

Atrasentan and other endothelin-A receptor antagonists reduce proteinuria by modulating glomerular hemodynamics and podocyte function. The ALIGN trial showed significant reduction in proteinuria in IgA nephropathy patients (Heerspink et al., 2024). Fluid retention remains a concern.

6.4 GLP-1 Receptor Agonists

The FLOW trial demonstrated that semaglutide reduced major kidney disease events by 24% in patients with type 2 diabetes and CKD (Perkovic et al., 2024), potentially positioning GLP-1 receptor agonists alongside SGLT2 inhibitors as standard nephroprotective therapy.

6.5 Regenerative Medicine and Bioartificial Kidneys

The Kidney Project, led by researchers at UCSF, has demonstrated proof-of-concept for bioartificial kidneys combining silicon nanopore membranes with cultured renal tubular cells for continuous, implantable dialysis (Roy et al., 2023). Stem cell therapies have shown promise in reducing renal fibrosis in animal models, though human trials remain in early phases.

7. Public Health Considerations

CKD disproportionately affects low- and middle-income countries, where access to dialysis and transplantation is severely limited. Screening strategies targeting high-risk populations using inexpensive tests — serum creatinine and urine dipstick — can identify CKD at earlier, more treatable stages. Cost-effectiveness analyses consistently support RAAS inhibitor therapy and SGLT2 inhibitors as high-value interventions that delay dialysis initiation.

8. Conclusion

Nephrology has evolved from a specialty defined by dialysis and transplantation to one increasingly focused on early detection and pharmacological prevention. The advent of SGLT2 inhibitors, non-steroidal MRAs, and GLP-1 receptor agonists represents a paradigm shift — for the first time, clinicians have multiple evidence-based therapies that meaningfully slow kidney function decline beyond traditional RAAS inhibition.

Future directions, including bioartificial kidneys, precision nephrology guided by genomic and proteomic profiling, and AI-assisted risk prediction, hold promise for further transforming kidney care. As the global prevalence of diabetes and hypertension continues to rise, nephrology's role in modern medicine will only grow more critical.
